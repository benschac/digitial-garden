## tl;dr

Treasure It was initially built for free item exchange. About 4 or 5 months ago, I added the ability to buy and sell to the app. I ended up needing to build my own internal ledger and currency functionality from scratch to handle double-sided accounting and app logic that depends on the amount of credits a user has.

This was our solution to pricing time.

## Intro

Treasure It takes a unique approach to commerce because our users have the luxury of meeting each other face-to-face for hyperlocal peer-to-peer exchange, rather than shipping something across the country.

This means paying in cash or Venmo, which is the preferred way to pay for most users while making us competitive with Facebook Marketplace and Craigslist.

But there needs to be a way for the platform to monetize and an incentive structure for people to show up and be courteous to others. Additionally, there needs to be a real financial incentive for people to be considerate of each other's time. That was the product thinking behind creating a ledger and a credit system.

## How does it work at a high level?

In Treasure It, buyers and sellers pay each other for items in person, and you use platform credits to pay for the match itself. Credits become the main policy and enforcement mechanism for taking action on the platform for both free items and items that are for sale.

## Policy

This is outside the scope of this blog post, but all credit actions are determined by policies. We organize and derive them via the [Functional Event Sourcing Decider](https://thinkbeforecoding.com/post/2021/12/17/functional-event-sourcing-decider) pattern from a set of events. Maybe it'll be a part two.

To make the rules below easier to follow, let's use **Alice** and **Bob**:

- **How a policy works:** When Alice or Bob takes an action, the policy looks at who's acting, what has already happened in the match, and whether a deadline has passed. It decides if the action is allowed and whose credits need to move.
- **What the ledger records:** Once the policy makes a call, the ledger records the credit grant, hold, refund, charge, or penalty.
- **Deadlines vs. background jobs:** A deadline passing and a background cron job processing it are two separate things. When Bob's 5-hour funding timer expires, the match is eligible to be cancelled right away, even if the worker that settles it runs every 5 minutes.
- **Milliunits:** One credit is `1,000` integer milliunits, so we never have to deal with floating-point math.
- **Base match fee (`F`):** `F` is one person's fee for a paid match (`Math.ceil(acceptedAmountCents / 2)` milliunits), saved on the offer thread as soon as the offer is accepted. If Alice buys a $20 item from Bob, `F` is 1 credit (`1,000` milliunits) for Alice and 1 credit for Bob. A `2F` penalty means twice that match's fee, which isn't always 2 credits.
- **Reserve, release, and consume:**
  - **Reserve** moves Alice's available credits into a hold so she can't spend them on another item at the same time.
  - **Release** moves held credits back to available if the match is cancelled under a rule that refunds her.
  - **Consume** spends the held credits for good once the pickup finishes (or when someone forfeits their hold).
  - **Penalties** can also debit available credits directly, even if that pushes a user's balance below zero.

## Credit use in free exchange

The biggest pain point in free exchange was users not getting back to one another in a timely fashion, constantly rescheduling, or just ghosting. This was the primary problem we were trying to solve with Treasure It from the start. Having an embedded schedule along with inventory and recipient management was step one.

Step two was using our credit and accounting system to put a financial cost on inconveniencing someone who's just trying to give something away.

Free exchange doesn't use the paid match fee **F**. Instead, say **Bob** is giving away a free item and **Alice** wants to pick it up:

| Situation                                | Policy and timing                                                                                                                                           | Credit effect                                                                                                                                          |
| ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Expressing interest or Flash claim       | Alice needs a positive available balance (anything above 0 milliunits) to raise her hand for Bob's free item.                                               | Balance check only. We don't reserve or charge credits just for expressing interest.                                                                   |
| Giver chooses a recipient                | Bob picks Alice and proposes future pickup times, opening one response timer per chosen item.                                                               | No immediate deduction.                                                                                                                                |
| Recipient response clock                 | Alice gets 240 working minutes to respond, counted between 8:00 AM and 9:45 PM `America/New_York`. The clock pauses overnight so late-night picks are fair. | No charge while Alice responds within that 4-working-hour window.                                                                                      |
| Halfway reminder                         | Sends Alice a reminder after 120 working minutes if she still hasn't picked a time.                                                                         | No credit movement.                                                                                                                                    |
| Valid response                           | Alice confirms one of Bob's times or sends back valid future availability. A plain chat message doesn't count as scheduling.                                | Answering on time avoids the penalty.                                                                                                                  |
| No response or late response             | Alice's 4-working-hour window expires without a valid scheduling response.                                                                                  | Debits 2 available credits **per item** from Alice, leaving her reserved credits alone. A late reply settles the timer but doesn't refund the penalty. |
| Selection is no longer needed            | Bob switches to another recipient, removes the item, or the pickup is already scheduled or finished.                                                        | Cancels Alice's response timer without charging her a penalty.                                                                                         |
| Existing selection with negative balance | Even if a timeout penalty pushes Alice into a negative balance, she can still finish scheduling an item Bob already picked her for.                         | Negative balance blocks Alice from claiming _new_ items, not finishing an active handoff.                                                              |
| Cancel or reschedule a free pickup       | Either Alice or Bob can reschedule, Alice can cancel, and Bob can remove items before completion.                                                           | No standalone cancellation or rescheduling fee on free items.                                                                                          |
| Complete a free pickup                   | A scheduled pickup can't be marked taken before its scheduled time.                                                                                         | No match fee is consumed on free pickups.                                                                                                              |
| Report a free pickup no-show             | Opens 10 minutes after the scheduled pickup time.                                                                                                           | Updates the pickup state and logs a reliability event, without the paid `10F` penalty.                                                                 |

#### What the ledger has to guarantee

| Rule                                             | Why it matters                                                                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Holds require available credits; penalties don't | Alice can't start a new match without enough available credits, but penalties (ghosting, bailing on a set pickup, or ignoring a free item handoff) can push a balance negative. |
| Balance and history always move together         | Every balance update and ledger row insert happens inside the same locked SQL transaction.                                                                                      |
| Retries are safe                                 | Re-running a reservation, purchase webhook, or timeout settlement with the same idempotency key returns the original result instead of charging or granting twice.              |
| Append-only history for normal operations        | App code never edits old ledger rows in place. Every change appends a new row explaining what moved and why.                                                                    |
| Notifications happen after commit                | Push notifications and follow-up jobs run only after the database transaction commits.                                                                                          |

## Why a single `credits` column wasn't enough

At first, a credit balance sounds like a single number on a user profile. Add to it when someone buys credits, subtract from it when they make a match.

That falls apart as soon as two people actually try to coordinate a pickup. When you accept a match, your credits need to be held so you can't spend them on three other items at the same time. If the other person backs out under a rule that refunds you, you should get those credits back. Only when the pickup happens are those credits gone for good.

A single `credits` column can't tell you which credits are locked up in an active match, why a balance dropped, or whether a Stripe webhook just fired twice. I built the credit ledger in Postgres to handle that lifecycle and connect the database math to what each person sees in their inbox.

<Callout title="Note on the title">

My original working title for this post was "Creating Double-Sided Accounting," which is still in the URL slug. When you reserve or release credits, the math moves cleanly between two columns, `available` and `reserved`. Buying or spending credits only updates the user's account without a matching platform account on the other side, so this is a credit ledger rather than full double-entry bookkeeping.

</Callout>

## Walking through a match

Say Alice buys three credits and accepts a match with Bob for a $20 item. The match costs one credit from each of them. Here is what happens to Alice's account from start to finish:

| Operation                        | Available credits | Reserved credits |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------: | ---------------: |
| Alice buys three credits         |                 3 |                0 |
| Reserve one for a match with Bob |                 2 |                1 |
| Release it when Bob cancels      |                 3 |                0 |
| Reserve one for a later match    |                 2 |                1 |
| Complete that pickup             |                 2 |                0 |

Reserving a credit is a hold. It stops Alice from double-spending that credit on another offer while keeping it around in case the match falls through and she's owed a refund. Completing the pickup spends the reserved credit.

If we only stored a single number, we'd constantly be guessing. Did Alice's balance drop because a pickup finished, because of a cancellation penalty, or because of a bug? Which match is holding her credit right now? Did we accidentally charge her twice?

The ledger answers those questions by recording every movement alongside the reason it happened.

## Keeping the balance and the paper trail together

Everything runs through two tables:

- `match_credit_accounts` holds each profile's current `available` and `reserved` balances.
- `match_credit_ledger` is the paper trail. Every time an account changes, we insert a row with the deltas, the balances after the move, the reason, the related purchase or offer thread, and an idempotency key.

Having a dedicated row in `match_credit_accounts` gives the app a fast way to read your current balance. More importantly, it gives Postgres a single row to lock when credits move.

**Note:** Both `match_credit_accounts` and `match_credit_ledger` point to `profiles` rather than having a direct foreign key between the account and ledger tables. ### Reading the ledger rows

Here is Alice's match from earlier, this time as rows in `match_credit_ledger`. We store credits as integer **milliunits**, with 1,000 milliunits per credit, so we never have to deal with floating-point math:

| Reason           | Available delta | Reserved delta | Available after | Reserved after |
| ---------------- | --------------: | -------------: | --------------: | -------------: |
| `purchase_grant` |           +3000 |              0 |            3000 |              0 |
| `reserve`        |           -1000 |          +1000 |            2000 |           1000 |
| `release`        |           +1000 |          -1000 |            3000 |              0 |
| `reserve`        |           -1000 |          +1000 |            2000 |           1000 |
| `consume`        |               0 |          -1000 |            2000 |              0 |

Notice how `reserve` and `release` move the exact same amount between `available` and `reserved`. `purchase_grant` adds to `available`, and `consume` drains `reserved` once the pickup is done.

As long as every balance change goes through the ledger functions, the account row always equals the sum of its ledger rows:

```text
account.available = sum(ledger.delta_available)
account.reserved  = sum(ledger.delta_reserved)
```

We keep those in sync by updating the account row and inserting the ledger row inside the same Postgres function and transaction.

## Locking the account row when reserving credits

Let's look at `reserve_match_credit`, the Postgres function that puts a hold on your credits.

When you call it, it makes sure your account row exists, locks that row, checks whether we've already processed this exact request, checks that you can afford the hold, updates your balances, and writes the ledger entry.

First things first, we lock the account row:

```sql
SELECT *
  INTO v_account
FROM public.match_credit_accounts
WHERE match_credit_accounts.profile_id = p_profile_id
FOR UPDATE;
```

Why lock the row? Say Alice has one credit left and tries to accept two different matches at the exact same time. Without `FOR UPDATE`, both requests could read `available_credits = 1000` in the app and let both holds through. With the row lock, the second transaction has to wait until the first one finishes and commits. By the time the second transaction reads the row, `available_credits` is `0`.

Next up, right after grabbing the lock, the function checks if there are enough credits and updates the account:

```sql
IF v_account.available_credits < v_credit_milliunits THEN
  RAISE EXCEPTION 'MATCH_CREDIT_INSUFFICIENT_CREDITS'
    USING ERRCODE = 'P0001';
END IF;

UPDATE public.match_credit_accounts AS account
SET
  available_credits = account.available_credits - v_credit_milliunits,
  reserved_credits = account.reserved_credits + v_credit_milliunits,
  updated_at = now()
WHERE account.profile_id = p_profile_id
RETURNING account.* INTO v_account;
```

The ledger `INSERT` happens right after this inside the same function. If writing the ledger row fails for any reason, Postgres rolls back the balance update too. We never update a balance in one query and hope a second history query succeeds.

When a transaction touches both Alice and Bob at once, like activating a match, the SQL function locks both `match_credit_accounts` rows sorted by `profile_id` so two concurrent requests can't deadlock each other. It also re-checks the offer state, accepted fee, and items inside the transaction. If anything changed between when the app read the match and when the SQL mutation ran, the database rejects the write.

## Making retries safe with idempotency keys

Networks drop and background jobs retry. An idempotency key lets the app safely repeat a call without charging someone twice.

Just checking "does this idempotency key already exist?" isn't enough, though. When `reserve_match_credit` finds an existing key, it also checks that the profile, offer thread, reason, and credit deltas match the original row. If caller code accidentally reuses an idempotency key for a different match or a different amount, Postgres throws a conflict error instead of silently pretending the new call worked.

When a retry is legitimate, the function returns the original ledger row ID alongside the account's **current** balances. Meanwhile, the ledger row itself keeps the `available_after` and `reserved_after` snapshot from when that move originally happened.

Webhook retries have a similar trap. It's tempting to use the Stripe webhook event ID as your idempotency key when someone buys credits, but Stripe can send more than one event type for the same payment. Instead, we log the webhook event ID separately and tie the credit grant key directly to the PaymentIntent:

```text
stripe:payment_intent:<id>
```

If the database commits the credit grant and the server crashes a millisecond later before returning `200` to Stripe, Stripe is going to send that webhook again. Keying the grant off the PaymentIntent means the retry sees the existing grant and exits cleanly instead of handing out double credits.

## Keeping cents and credit milliunits straight

Item prices and credits use different units. Item prices are stored in integer cents, while the ledger stores credits in integer milliunits, even on a couple of older columns whose names still end in `_credits`.

Here is the function that calculates the base credit fee from an accepted offer price:

```ts
export const resolveBaseMatchFeeCreditMilliunits = (
  acceptedAmountCents: number,
): number => Math.ceil(acceptedAmountCents / 2);
```

For a $20 item, `2000` cents comes out to `1000` milliunits, or 1 credit per person. For $20.01, `2001` cents rounds up to `1001` milliunits.

When an offer is accepted, we snapshot that result into `base_match_fee_credit_milliunits` on the offer thread. When the pickup finishes days later, settlement reads that snapshot rather than recalculating the fee. That way, if we ever change our fee formula, we won't pull the rug out from under matches that are already in progress.

## Pulling marketplace rules into pure functions

Getting the SQL math right was only half the problem. Early on, marketplace rules were scattered across the codebase, and testing them was a pain. What a buyer, seller, or giver is allowed to do next depends on both the action they're taking and the current state of the match.

I didn't want to spin up database fixtures and mock push notifications just to test whether cancelling a pickup should refund a credit. Earlier I mentioned the decider pattern we use for policies. Without getting into every rule here, I pulled those decisions into pure TypeScript functions built around a `decide -> evolve -> react` loop:

1. `decide` takes the current state, a command, and context, and returns either a rejection or a list of typed events.
2. `evolveAll` folds those events into the next state.
3. `reactions` turns those events into side effects like notifications.

Here is how those pieces fit together once `decide` accepts a command:

```ts
return {
  kind: "accepted",
  state,
  nextState: evolveAll(state, decision.events),
  events: decision.events,
  effects: decision.events.flatMap((event) =>
    reactions(event, decisionContext),
  ),
  decisionContext,
};
```

Because `decide`, `evolveAll`, and `reactions` are pure functions, unit tests can throw weird state combinations at them in milliseconds. Credit events describe who gets charged or refunded, why, and how many milliunits to move, without touching balance fields directly.

Once the policy layer decides what should happen, the service layer calls the Postgres function with the expected state and an idempotency key. Only after the database transaction commits do we fire off the notification effects.

## Connecting the ledger to the inbox

A match has two people in it, and at any given moment one person usually needs to act while the other waits. Showing a generic "Accepted" badge to both Alice and Bob isn't helpful.

Take credit funding right after an offer is accepted. To accept an offer, Alice has to have enough credits in her account right then. But what if Bob, who originally sent the offer, doesn't have enough credits yet?

When that happens, we reserve Alice's credits, lock the items to the match, and put the thread into `awaiting_credits`. That gives Bob five hours to buy credits and finish funding his side of the match before pickup scheduling can move forward.

On the server, `toActionView` figures out whether the match is waiting on Alice or Bob, which buttons each of them should see, what happens if either one backs out now, and when the current deadline expires. The inbox turns that into `ActivityInboxRowViewModel`, a discriminated union keyed by `kind`, with cases like `offer_pickup_confirmation`, `offer_scheduled_pickup`, and the funding states.

Because each `kind` carries only the fields for that specific state, the UI can switch on `row.kind` and render the right call to action without piecing together a bunch of boolean flags.

One bug we had to guard against here was leftover scheduling data on finished pickups. The inbox mapper always checks whether the pickup is already completed before looking at scheduling fields, so an old timestamp can't make a finished match look like it still needs action.

## Cleaning up unfinished settlements

Sometimes a pickup finishes, the state transitions, and the credit settlement step needs a retry. To catch those, I set up a Trigger.dev job that runs every 15 minutes, queries Postgres for completed pickups with unfinished credit work, and steps through them one by one so a failure on one match doesn't block the rest of the batch.

At the end of the day, keeping the accounts, the ledger, and the state checks inside Postgres has been the right call for Treasure It. When someone asks why their balance changed or what a match is waiting on, I don't have to dig through application logs to piece the story together. It's all sitting right there in `match_credit_ledger`. Hopefully this helps anyone building a similar credit or reservation system in their own app.
